05_04 The Bootstrap and Monte Carlo

Estimate uncertainty by resampling the data, and answer hard questions by simulating a model.

Key ideas

Bootstrap: draw $B$ resamples of size $n$ with replacement, compute $T^*_b$; $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}=\mathrm{sd}(T^*)$; percentile interval $[T^*_{(0.025)},T^*_{(0.975)}]$, basic interval $[2T-T^*_{(0.975)},\,2T-T^*_{(0.025)}]$, BCa corrects bias and skew. For the mean the ideal bootstrap SE is $\sqrt{\tfrac{n-1}{n}}\,s/\sqrt n$. Monte Carlo from a known model: $\hat p=\frac1B\sum\mathbf 1\{\cdot\}$ with error $\sqrt{p(1-p)/B}$; $B\approx(1.96)^2p(1-p)/\varepsilon^2$ for $\pm\varepsilon$. It fails for extremes, tiny samples, infinite variance and dependent data.

In plain words

  • The sample is a miniature of the population: resample it (with replacement) to see how much an estimate would jump around.
  • No formula needed: try the median, 90th percentile or trimmed mean in the lab; the same code works.
  • Monte Carlo is the same idea for a model you wrote down: simulate it many times and count. The error shrinks like 1/sqrt(B).
  • Know the failure cases: choose the maximum in the lab and see the spike that the real sampling distribution does not have.

Where it is used

Statistics

Standard errors and intervals for medians, percentiles, ratios and correlations; permutation tests.

Data science

A/B tests with relative lifts, uncertainty bands for KPIs, risk and capacity simulation.

ML and AI

Bagging and random forests (out-of-bag error), bootstrapping test metrics, Monte Carlo methods in Bayesian inference.
