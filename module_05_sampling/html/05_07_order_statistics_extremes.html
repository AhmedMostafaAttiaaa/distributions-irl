05_07 Order Statistics and Extremes

The minimum, the maximum, the median and other quantiles of a sample, and the three laws of extremes.

Key ideas

Order statistics $X_{(1)}\le\dots\le X_{(n)}$. Max: $F^n$; min: $1-(1-F)^n$; $k$-th: $F(X_{(k)})\sim\mathrm{Beta}(k,n-k+1)$, mean $k/(n+1)$. Distribution-free CI for the quantile $\xi_p$: $[X_{(l)},X_{(u)}]$ with coverage $P(l\le B\le u-1)$, $B\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n,p)$. Median: $\mathrm{Var}\approx1/(4nf(\xi)^2)$. Extremes converge to Gumbel ($\xi=0$), Fréchet ($\xi>0$) or Weibull ($\xi<0$); the $T$-year return level is $F_{\text{year}}^{-1}(1-1/T)$; the max of $n$ Exponentials is about $\ln n$ plus a Gumbel.

In plain words

  • The max stays low only if every draw does, so it creeps up with n: like $\ln n$ for light tails, much faster for heavy tails.
  • Any order statistic is a Beta on the probability scale, whatever the parent: try the three parents in the lab and watch the same shape.
  • Quantile intervals need no assumptions: just count how many points fall below the true quantile, a Binomial.
  • A 100-year level from 50 years of data is shaky: use the simulator in Example 3 to see how wide the error really is.

Where it is used

Statistics

Distribution-free confidence intervals for medians and quantiles, rank tests, Q-Q plots, flood, wind and loss modelling.

Data science

Tail latency (p99) of pages that call many services, capacity planning, robust summaries and outlier detection.

ML and AI

Max pooling, best-of-n sampling and beam search, selection bias when picking the best of K models, extreme-value methods for the tails of losses and gradients.
