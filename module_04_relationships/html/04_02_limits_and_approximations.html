04_02 Limits and Approximations: How Good, and When?

Measure the error of each classic approximation instead of trusting a rule of thumb.

Key ideas

Binomial $\to$ Poisson (error at most $np^2$, Le Cam), Binomial / Poisson / Gamma $\to$ Normal (error governed by skewness, Berry-Esseen $\le0.4748\frac{p^2+(1-p)^2}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}$), chi-square $\to$ Normal (Wilson-Hilferty: $(\chi^2_\nu/\nu)^{1/3}\approx N(1-\frac2{9\nu},\frac2{9\nu})$), t $\to$ Normal, Hypergeometric $\to$ Binomial. For a discrete count always use the continuity correction $P(X\le k)\approx\Phi\big(\frac{k+0.5-\mu}{\sigma}\big)$. Pick a pair with the first slider (1 Binomial~Normal, 2 Binomial~Poisson, 3 Gamma~Normal, 4 t~Normal); the main chart overlays the approximation on the exact distribution and reports the Kolmogorov distance.

In plain words

  • An approximation is a trade: a simpler curve for a small error. The error depends on how lopsided (skewed) the exact distribution is.
  • Rare events: few trials of a tiny probability give a lopsided Binomial; use the Poisson (or the exact Binomial), not the Normal (Example 1).
  • Small samples: 1.96 is too small; with 5 observations a 95 % interval built with it only covers about 88 % of the time (Example 2).
  • Add half a unit when you replace a count by a Normal, and use the cube-root trick for chi-square and Gamma (Example 3).

Where it is used

Statistics

Large-sample z tests and intervals, exact versus approximate tests, chi-square and t critical values.

Data science

A/B tests with few conversions, alert thresholds for event counts, quick capacity estimates.

ML and AI

Gaussian approximations of posteriors and of gradient noise, Poisson models for sparse counts.
